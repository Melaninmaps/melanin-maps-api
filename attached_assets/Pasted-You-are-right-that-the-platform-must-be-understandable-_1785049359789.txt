You are right that the platform must be understandable at a glance **and** defensible when someone wants the deeper explanation.

My recommendation is:

> **Use a simple color-and-label system, but do not reduce everything to one universal “Safety Score.”**

A single number such as **80% safe** sounds clear, but it may falsely combine very different things:

* Historical sundown-town status
* Recent discrimination reports
* General crime data
* Member comfort
* Community support
* Accessibility
* Day versus night experience
* Quantity and quality of available evidence

A place might have strong community support and active cultural organizations while also having recent discrimination concerns. One score could hide that tension.

## The better model: a clear headline with expandable detail

The map pin could communicate three things immediately:

### Color

* **Red:** Strong concern or caution
* **Amber:** Mixed, limited, or changing information
* **Green:** More reassuring recent indicators
* **Gray:** Not enough current information

But color must never stand alone. W3C accessibility guidance requires information conveyed through color to also be communicated through text, shape, symbols, or another visual means. ([W3C][1])

### Plain-language label

Use labels such as:

* **Use Extra Caution**
* **Mixed Community Information**
* **More Reassuring Recent Information**
* **Not Enough Information Yet**
* **Historical Context Available**

### Evidence strength

A smaller line could say:

* **High confidence**
* **Moderate confidence**
* **Limited information**

That helps a user understand whether the result reflects substantial evidence or only a few data points.

## What the pin might look like

### At-a-glance version

**Red pin — Use Extra Caution**

> Historical exclusion documented
> Recent discrimination reports increased
> Moderate evidence

Then:

**Why this result?**
**Learn more**

### Another example

**Amber pin — Mixed Community Information**

> Strong local community engagement
> Some recent discrimination concerns
> Limited recent reporting

### Historical data without present-day evidence

**Gray-and-amber pin — Historical Context Available**

> Historically documented sundown town
> Not enough current information to characterize conditions today

This is much more honest than assigning a red **35% safety score** solely because of historical data.

# Could you still use a number?

Yes, but I would make the number describe the **information**, not claim to measure absolute safety.

Better possibilities:

### Community Confidence: 80%

Meaning:

> We have enough recent, corroborated information to speak with relatively high confidence.

That is not the same as saying a place is 80% safe.

### Current Community Signal: 72/100

This could reflect current reported experiences, but only after the formula is mature, legally reviewed, and thoroughly tested.

### Evidence Available: 80%

This tells users how complete the information is rather than making a broad promise about safety.

For early versions, I prefer:

> **Label + color + evidence level**

over a percentage.

A percentage feels scientifically precise even when the underlying data may be incomplete, self-selected, unevenly distributed, or outdated.

# Historical Sundown Towns need a separate symbol

Do not let a historical designation automatically determine the present-day color.

For example:

* A distinct **history marker** or symbol identifies historical sundown-town research.
* A separate color indicates recent community information.
* The detail view explains both.

A town could therefore show:

**Historical Context:** Confirmed sundown-town history
**Current Community Signal:** Limited information
**Recent Reports:** No meaningful trend available

Or:

**Historical Context:** Probable historical record
**Current Community Signal:** Use Extra Caution
**Why:** Multiple approved recent discrimination reports

That prevents history from being erased while avoiding unsupported claims about today.

# The experience should have three levels of explanation

This is how you serve a 13-year-old, a 26-year-old, and a person with two doctorates through the same interface.

## Level 1 — Immediate understanding

Visible directly on the pin or card:

> **Use Extra Caution**

> Recent discrimination concerns

No jargon.

## Level 2 — Why

One tap:

> This result reflects several approved reports of discriminatory treatment during the past 12 months and limited recent evidence of community support.

## Level 3 — Learn More

For users who want depth:

* Historical sources
* Report date ranges
* Evidence categories
* Number of qualifying reports
* Whether reports were corroborated
* Community organizations
* Positive engagement and reconciliation work
* Data limitations
* Methodology explanation
* Correction process

The CDC’s Clear Communication Index supports designing public-facing information around everyday language and the characteristics that help people understand important material. ([CDC][2])

That is not “dumbing it down.” It is **progressive disclosure**: the main message is immediately understandable, while greater depth remains available.

# Recommended future classification

I would propose these four member-facing statuses:

| Status                                 | Color | Meaning                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------- | ----- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Use Extra Caution**                  | Red   | Meaningful recent concerns supported by sufficient evidence                  |
| **Mixed Community Information**        | Amber | Positive and concerning signals coexist, or conditions appear to be changing |
| **More Reassuring Recent Information** | Green | Recent information is generally reassuring, while never guaranteeing safety  |
| **Not Enough Information Yet**         | Gray  | Insufficient current information to make a responsible characterization      |

Historical context should be an additional marker:

> **Historical Exclusion Record**

It should not be a fifth safety grade.

## I would avoid these labels

* Safe
* Unsafe
* Dangerous
* 100% Safe
* Low-risk people
* Bad neighborhood
* Good neighborhood

No platform can guarantee safety, and those labels can stigmatize communities or imply certainty the evidence does not support.

# Weighting principle

The status should draw from separate dimensions, not one undifferentiated count:

* Recent discrimination reports
* Corroboration and evidence quality
* Recency
* Day/night differences
* Community support and intervention
* Community organizations and resources
* Positive community engagement
* Documented public findings
* Historical context
* Documented reconciliation or corrective activity
* Data completeness

The internal weights and thresholds may be valuable trade-secret methodology. The public should understand the **categories of information used**, but you do not have to publish the exact formula that competitors could copy or people could manipulate.

However, the system must remain auditable internally and understandable enough externally that a community can challenge an inaccurate result.

# My recommendation for Build 97

For Build 97:

* Add the **Historical Sundown Towns** layer.
* Use historical evidence/status labels.
* Keep historical context separate from present-day safety.
* Replace “Safety Score” with **Community Safety Insights** or **Community Insights**.
* Use plain-language labels and icons.
* Do not launch a numerical present-day score until the evidence model, weighting, minimum thresholds, appeals process, and disclaimers are complete.
* Show **Not Enough Information Yet** rather than inventing a neutral score.
* Make **Why this result?** available where any current-status label is displayed.

The color system can be part of Build 97 only where the underlying meaning is honest and limited. For the historical sundown-town layer, I would use status-specific history symbols rather than red/green danger coloring unless recent evidence separately supports a caution label.

The guiding design principle should be:

> **Simple first. Clear second. Detailed when requested. Never falsely certain.**

That meets people where they are without alienating or patronizing anyone.
