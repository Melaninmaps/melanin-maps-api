Yes — I would add this **now** as a dedicated Library discovery layer, because it connects directly to the ownership taxonomy, Kinfolk, business discovery, travel, cultural sites, events, heritage, food, and the local-language work we have already been building.

I would **not label the user-facing section “Minorities.”** My recommendation is:

## **Culture & Roots**

**Explore where you're from, what you grew up around, and the communities you want to stay connected to.**

Then give the user multiple ways in:

**Region → Country/Territory → Culture/Community → Language → Local/Diaspora experience**

That way someone can search broadly for **Caribbean**, narrowly for **Jamaican**, or later even more specifically for **Maroon history**, **Haitian Creole resources**, **Afro-Cuban culture**, etc., without forcing every person into one umbrella identity.

For Africa specifically, I checked the African Union rather than relying on memory. The AU currently lists **55 Member States divided into Central, Eastern, Northern, Southern, and Western Africa**, and the country grouping above follows that official framework. ([African Union][1]) The difference people sometimes encounter is that the UN has **54 African Member States**, while the AU's 55-member framework includes the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic; that distinction should be documented rather than silently “fixed” by Replit. ([Africa Renewal][2])

I also deliberately made **North Africa dual-discoverable under both Africa and MENA**. Someone from Morocco should not have the app implicitly tell them they have to stop being African to search through a MENA lens, or vice versa. That is exactly the kind of flattening this Library should avoid.

And this should eventually connect beautifully to the flywheel: a member may start with **“my family is from Ghana”**, save Ghanaian businesses, follow Ghanaian cultural content, watch a heritage video, attend an event, and ask Kinfolk about visiting Accra. Kinfolk gradually gets better because the member gave it that connection—not because Kinfolk guessed who they were.
