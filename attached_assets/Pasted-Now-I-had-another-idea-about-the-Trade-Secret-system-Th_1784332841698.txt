Now... I had another idea about the Trade Secret system.
The more I think about Mapping With Melanin™, the more I believe the scoring shouldn't be a single algorithm at all.
It should be an ecosystem.
Google has PageRank.
Uber has driver/rider ratings.
Airbnb has reviews.
You have an opportunity to create something much richer.
I would call it the:
Mapping With Melanin™ Trust & Belonging Framework
The Community Trust Engine is one part of it.
Inside that framework are multiple confidential models.
For example:
Layer 1 — Identity Confidence
"Do we know who this is?"
Inputs might include:
Verification level
Account maturity
Security checks
Business documentation
Consistency
Layer 2 — Contribution Quality
"Do they add value?"
Not quantity.
Quality.
Helpful reviews.
Thoughtful comments.
Community participation.
Educational posts.
Supporting businesses.
Attending events.
Hosting events.
Layer 3 — Community Relationships
This is one I haven't mentioned before.
Instead of asking:
How active are they?
Ask:
How connected are they?
For example:
Do businesses interact with them?
Do they support multiple businesses?
Do they participate across different communities?
Are they introducing people to one another?
Are they strengthening the network?
That's a network effect, not just an activity count.
Layer 4 — Reliability
Do they consistently behave in a trustworthy way over time?
Not one good month.
Not one viral post.
Consistency.
Layer 5 — Positive Impact
This might become one of your most unique measurements.
Not:
How many followers?
Not:
How many likes?
Instead:
How much positive impact did they create?
For example:
Introduced a new business to the community.
Helped answer questions.
Organized events.
Welcomed newcomers.
Shared useful safety information.
Connected people with opportunities.
That's a very different philosophy.
Here's the part I think is the secret sauce
I would never allow any single category to account for more than about 25–30% of a composite score.
Why?
Because then the system becomes difficult to game.
Someone can't simply:
Attend 1,000 events.
Or leave 5,000 comments.
Or invite 500 friends.
And dominate.
Instead, they must demonstrate value across multiple dimensions.
That makes manipulation much harder.
I also think there should be "negative weights"
Not punishments.
Corrections.
For example:
Suppose someone leaves:
200 reviews.
But they're consistently flagged as inaccurate.
That should reduce the influence of future reviews.
Not necessarily their standing in the community—but the weight assigned to their future contributions until they rebuild trust.
That's a healthier model than simply banning or ignoring people.
Businesses should work similarly
A business shouldn't become influential just because it pays for a membership.
Instead, influence should be earned through behaviors like:
Consistent responsiveness.
Community engagement.
Hosting or supporting events.
Collaborating with other businesses.
Positive experiences over time.
Verified ownership.
Maintaining accurate information.
Again, no single factor should dominate.
Something I think deserves its own model
One thing we haven't talked about is Community Momentum.
Imagine if a business suddenly becomes much more active:
Hosting events.
Responding to customers.
Supporting nonprofits.
Collaborating with ambassadors.
The platform could recognize that positive momentum.
Not because it's permanent.
Because it's happening now.
Likewise, if a community becomes less active over time, that could be reflected appropriately.
Momentum rewards positive change without erasing long-term trust.
One thing I would avoid
I would avoid displaying too many scores publicly.
Users don't need to see:
Trust: 87.4
Contribution: 91.8
Reliability: 84.2
That encourages people to optimize for numbers.
Instead, the internal models can drive what the user experiences:
Better recommendations.
More trusted reviews.
Stronger community signals.
Higher-quality discovery.
The framework works behind the scenes without turning the app into a game.
