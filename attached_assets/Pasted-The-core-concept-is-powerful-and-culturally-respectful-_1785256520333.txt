The core concept is powerful and culturally respectful, but the **current voice implementation needs guardrails before it should ever speak this way automatically**.

## What is strong

The history-first approach is excellent. The registry does more than collect slang: it explains why places and phrases matter, connects institutions and neighborhoods to present-day culture, and treats Black cities as living communities rather than tourist products. The 36-city registry contains city-specific vocabulary, cultural touchstones, transit references, neighborhoods, and writing guidance—not just generic destination copy. 

The strongest entries are those where Kinfolk carries historical context underneath a warm, contemporary voice:

* Tulsa and Greenwood.
* Durham and Parrish Street.
* Montgomery and the Civil Rights Movement.
* Savannah and Gullah Geechee heritage.
* Charleston and Mother Emanuel.
* Cincinnati and the Ohio River freedom corridor.
* Jacksonville and LaVilla.

That is distinctive and worth preserving.

## What could offend users

The risk is not the use of AAVE itself. The risk is presenting **one narrow, youth-heavy slang register as the voice of an entire Black city**.

Not every Black person speaks AAVE, and many speakers shift between AAVE and other varieties depending on setting, age, profession, and audience. Dialect labels describe broad patterns; they do not mean every member of a community speaks identically. ([Yale Grammatical Diversity Project][1])

Several current navigation examples feel more like performance or imitation than natural assistance:

* “On gang” and “slime” for Atlanta.
* “Young bull” directed automatically at a Philadelphia user.
* “Opps” and “lick” in the Chicago travel vocabulary.
* “Faded” in Los Angeles.
* “Ghost ride” as a general Oakland cultural cue.
* “Bama” in Washington, DC.
* “Finna” inserted into nearly every city regardless of speaker or context.

Some of those expressions may be locally recognizable, but they vary by generation, neighborhood, social group, and meaning. They should be **available contextual vocabulary**, not mandatory phrases Kinfolk forces into sentences.

AAVE is also much more than vocabulary. It includes systematic grammar, aspect, emphasis, discourse practices, rhythm, humor, and meaning distinctions—for example habitual **be**, stressed **BIN**, perfective **done**, and **finna**. Treating it mainly as a rotating slang list risks reducing a rule-governed language variety to catchphrases. ([Yale Grammatical Diversity Project][2])

## Specific language I would flag for expert or community review

### Replace automated “Minority” substitutions

Phrases such as:

* “Minority mecca”
* “Brooklyn Minority excellence”
* “oldest Minority neighborhood”
* “Minority culture was born here”

read like an automated replacement of **Black** with **Minority**. That weakens historical accuracy and can sound evasive.

Where the history is specifically Black, say **Black** or **African American**. Broader inclusion should be added through additional profiles, not by replacing a precise identity with a generic umbrella term.

### Tuskegee attribution

Use the full or shortened accurate name:

> **The U.S. Public Health Service Untreated Syphilis Study at Tuskegee**

Avoid language that makes Tuskegee University or the city appear to have owned or conducted the abuse. Descendants and public-health authorities have specifically objected to placing responsibility on “Tuskegee” rather than the federal agency. ([CDC][3])

### Black Masking Indians

The files contain awkward wording such as “Minority Masking Indians.” That appears to be another bad substitution. This should receive direct New Orleans community review and use the community’s current preferred terminology rather than an automated replacement.

### Historical superlatives

Claims such as:

* “oldest Black church in North America”
* “largest majority-Black city in the South”
* “largest Black exposition in the United States”
* “40% of enslaved Africans entered through Sullivan’s Island”

should have citations and review dates. They may be supportable, but statements of “oldest,” “largest,” or precise percentages should not live indefinitely in a prompt without source tracking.

## The safest voice model

Kinfolk should have several independent controls:

**Cultural grounding**

* Historically informed
* Community-aware
* Regional references enabled

**Language register**

* Standard/conversational
* AAVE-informed
* Strong local voice

**Tone**

* Warm
* Professional
* Casual
* Playful

**Profanity**

* None by default
* Mild, explicit opt-in
* Unfiltered, separate explicit opt-in

A user selecting AAVE should **not** automatically receive profanity, youth slang, flirtation, gendered language, or terms such as “young bull,” “slime,” “bro,” or “fam.”

For directions, I would make the default local voice recognizable but restrained:

> “Iight, your left turn is coming up at MLK Drive.”

rather than:

> “Iight, hang a left at that jawn on MLK, young bull—you almost there, ard?”

The fuller version can exist as an optional preview under something like **Strong Local Voice**, after the user hears a sample and affirmatively selects it.

## Cities I would add for diaspora expansion

I would not immediately add another 36. Start with a small second cohort that demonstrates several diaspora models well.

### First expansion cohort

**Miami–Fort Lauderdale, Florida — Caribbean and Latin American diaspora**
Build distinct layers for Haitian, Jamaican, Bahamian, Cuban, Dominican, Afro-Latino, and broader Caribbean communities. Do not collapse them into one “Miami multicultural” voice. Greater Miami is one of the leading U.S. destinations for Haitian immigrants and also a major Brazilian destination. ([Migration Policy Institute][4])

**Washington, DC–Silver Spring–Northern Virginia — Ethiopian and East African diaspora**
Your DC Black American profile should remain. Add a separate Ethiopian/Eritrean/East African community layer with community validation, relevant neighborhoods, businesses, religious institutions, foodways, and migration history.

**New York City — Dominican, Puerto Rican, Caribbean, West African and South Asian layers**
New York should become the model for a city containing several parallel cultural communities rather than one blended voice. The existing Black New York profile remains; users can explore Dominican New York, Puerto Rican New York, Haitian New York, West African New York, and others.

**Boston–Cambridge–Brockton, Massachusetts — Haitian, Cape Verdean and Brazilian diaspora**
Boston is particularly valuable because the communities should remain distinct: Cape Verdean history is not Brazilian history, and neither is interchangeable with Haitian identity. Greater Boston is one of the largest destinations for both Haitian and Brazilian immigrants. ([Migration Policy Institute][4])

**Minneapolis–Saint Paul, Minnesota — Somali, Oromo, Ethiopian and Hmong communities**
This would demonstrate that Mapping With Melanin™ understands diaspora beyond the East Coast and beyond only food and festivals. It should include entrepreneurship, civic leadership, faith, youth culture, education, and community resources.

**Orlando–Kissimmee, Florida — Puerto Rican, Brazilian and broader Latin American communities**
Orlando is a major Brazilian destination and has a strong Puerto Rican community. It could support family travel, relocation, small businesses, cultural events, and professional networks. ([migrationpolicy.org][5])

**Newark–Elizabeth, New Jersey — Brazilian, Portuguese-speaking, Afro-Latino and Caribbean communities**
This would be a strong business and neighborhood ecosystem city, especially for immigrant-owned businesses, legal services, food, faith communities, and intergenerational stories.

**Houston, Texas — Nigerian, West African, Latino and Vietnamese communities**
Keep the current Black Houston profile. Add separate community layers rather than rewriting it as generically diverse.

**Los Angeles, California — Mexican American, Korean, Filipino, Armenian, Ethiopian and Central American communities**
Los Angeles is the clearest example of why one city cannot have one cultural voice. Kinfolk should first understand which community, neighborhood, and context the user is asking about.

**Philadelphia, Pennsylvania — Puerto Rican, Dominican, West African, Caribbean and Southeast Asian communities**
This is especially important because Philadelphia is your home launch context. It allows the app to demonstrate that Black Philadelphia remains foundational while the city experience visibly includes other communities.

### Later cohorts

* San Francisco–Oakland: Filipino, Chinese, Latino, Pacific Islander and Black Bay Area histories.
* Providence–Pawtucket: Dominican, Cape Verdean and Portuguese-speaking communities.
* New Bedford–Fall River: Cape Verdean and Portuguese diaspora.
* Chicago: Mexican, Puerto Rican, Polish, South Asian and East African layers alongside Black Chicago.
* Dallas–Fort Worth: Nigerian, Ethiopian, South Asian, Latino and Vietnamese communities.
* Seattle–Tacoma: East African, Filipino, Pacific Islander and Asian diaspora communities.
* Dearborn–Detroit: Arab American and MENA history alongside the existing Black Detroit profile.
* San Juan, Puerto Rico: only when the platform is prepared for Spanish-first cultural governance rather than translating an English profile.

## The most important structural decision

Do not create a single “diaspora voice” for each city.

Use:

> **City → Community → Neighborhood → Context → User preference**

For example:

**Miami**

* Black Miami / Overtown
* Haitian Miami / Little Haiti
* Cuban Miami / Little Havana
* Bahamian Miami / Coconut Grove history
* Brazilian South Florida
* LGBTQIA+ Miami

Each layer needs its own contributors, sources, pronunciation guidance, sensitive terms, and review process.
