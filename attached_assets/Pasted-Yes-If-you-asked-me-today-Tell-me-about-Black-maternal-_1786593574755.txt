### Black Maternal Mortality in the United States

Black maternal mortality refers to the disproportionately high rate at which Black women die from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth in the United States. It is one of the country's most persistent racial health disparities.

The latest finalized CDC maternal-mortality data currently available show that in **2024, non-Hispanic Black women died from maternal causes at a rate of 44.8 deaths per 100,000 live births**, compared with **14.2 for non-Hispanic White women** — more than three times the rate. ([CDC Blogs][1])

And an important part of understanding this issue is that **race itself is not the biological explanation**. NIH describes these disparities as arising from social and structural inequities, including differences in access to high-quality care, rather than intrinsic racial biological differences. ([Office of Disease Prevention][2])

The disparity also isn't adequately explained by education or socioeconomic achievement. CDC research has found racial disparities across education levels; in historical pregnancy-related mortality data, Black women with a college education or higher experienced dramatically higher pregnancy-related mortality than similarly educated White women. ([CDC Archive][3])

### Why does this happen?

There isn't one cause. Research points to interacting factors that include **differences in quality of care, structural racism, implicit bias, access to appropriate care, and whether symptoms and concerns are recognized and acted upon quickly**. CDC also notes that hospitals serving higher proportions of Black women have historically had poorer maternal outcomes. ([CDC][4])

Patient experience matters too. In CDC survey data, about **30% of Black women reported mistreatment during maternity care**, compared with about 20% of women overall in the survey. Almost half of all respondents reported holding back from asking questions or sharing concerns during maternity care. ([CDC][5])

There are also specific medical conditions involved. Cardiovascular conditions, infection, hemorrhage and other complications contribute to pregnancy-related deaths, and CDC research has found racial differences in the contribution of conditions including cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. ([CDC][6])

### The especially troubling part: many deaths are preventable

The CDC reports that **more than 80% of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States are preventable**. That means this isn't simply a discussion about unavoidable complications of pregnancy. Timely recognition of warning signs, appropriate treatment, continuity of care and respectful, high-quality maternity care can save lives. ([CDC][7])

And maternal health doesn't end when someone leaves the delivery room. The CDC defines pregnancy-related deaths as deaths occurring during pregnancy or **within one year after the pregnancy ends** when related to or aggravated by the pregnancy. ([CDC Archive][8])

### What this means for Black women and families

The statistics shouldn't be interpreted as “Black women should be afraid to become pregnant.” A better takeaway is that Black mothers deserve **responsive, respectful and evidence-based care**, and that concerns during pregnancy and postpartum should be taken seriously.
